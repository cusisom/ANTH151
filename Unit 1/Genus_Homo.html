The Genus Homo

We left off in our last discussion by considering the diet and morphological shift in Gracile Australopithecines. It’s wild that that part of my lectures had to change as recently as 2025. The overturn of our commonly held belief that the encephalization jump in Au. africanus resulted from increased consumption of animal protein (Brand-Miller et al. (2026); Lüdecke et al. (2025); Sponheimer and Lee-Thorp (1999)) has shifted the paradigm on early human evolution. But the science is always changing. Who knows what the next round of research will unveil.

As stated last week, this overturn was difficult to swallow in part because of how well the original theory matched the evidence. There is no question that the masticatory and digestive systems of this species reduced in size and power. It is further evident that brain growth began to trend positively with this shift, leading to increased encephalization. We can also be farily certain that these combined changes are a result of a new dietary niche. I guess with the way things stand, we must accept that increased omnivory with the inclusion of more glucose and insect protein was enough to kick this trend into gear. That being said, we have much stronger evidence for meat-eating (carnivory) in the hominins which evolve from the gracile australopithecine line.

This last section of Unit 1 focuses on the evolution of our genus and, ultimately, our species. Even more than our last discussion, however, interpretations of this fossil record illustrate an academic battleground. Almost every aspect of these chapters reflects controversy and debate. As you read through the textbook assignment you will come to see that much of the disagreement centers on a couple of general themes and perspectives in paleoanthropology. These amount to the opposing perspectives of taxonomic lumpers and splitters, the acceptance of grade versus clade categories, and the diagnosis of stone tools versus manufactured stone tool industries. We will cover each of these themes in this supplemental text. But first, a couple of general notes about our genus.

The genus Homo is commonly defined by an increase in carnivory, use and manufacture of stone tools, and decreasing robusticity coincident with increased encephalization. The most widely accepted species in the genus include Homo habilis, Homo erectus, Homo neanderthalensis, and Homo sapiens (though see Table 1). Despite the ongoing contention, most authors agree that, in relation to australopithecines, Homo was/is more adaptable and highly cultural. The notion of cumulative culture encapsulates the shift. Once our ancestors begin manufacturing tools and processing food (cutting, grinding, cooking, et cetera) there occurs a positive feedback loop where we begin to learn how to manipulate our enviornment at an exponential rate. This is the legacy of our genus. The debates, then, do not so much relate to what was significant about Homo or even the evolutionary advance of the genus but focus instead on how we delineate and the genus and differentiate the species within.

Theory and Philosophy: Splitters vs. Lumpers and Grade vs. Clades

The most poignant undertones of your assigned reading reflect different philosophies in paleoanthropology. It is worth noting that we (as people) make up the categories applied to the world around us. Outside of this discussion, you can find heated debate regarding whether or not our categories are real at all (is the classification of a species grounded in reality? Is the divide between species real?). This is not to say that the taxa do not reflect a certain degree of biological truth, but the dividing lines between these units are determined by a governing body of biologists (such as the International Commission on Zoological Nomenclature or ICZN). I mention this simply to illustrate that, in these debates, there really cannot be a right or wrong opinion, only an accepted or unaccepted opinion. Now we can dive into these different approaches.

To split or not to split

You have already read a bit on this topic in Chapter 9 of Explorations (allegedly). The opposing philosophies of Lumpers and Splitters regard how researchers divide fossil populations by their observed morphology. One side of the debate considers how variable species can be when spread over a wide geographic range or influenced by different environments. These are the Lumpers. They suggest that subtle differences in morphology are inherent in most, if not all species-level populations, and that they are relatively minor modifications to the overall species form. Splitters, on the other hand, tend to focus on morphological insights into reproductive isolation (see BSC below). They suggest that any unique set of traits which can be population specific qualifies as diagnostic criteria for species classification. As stated in the book, the philosophies differ on how variation is interpreted. Check out Figure 3 to see examples of how these taxonomic problems take shape.

While this debate occurs in neontological discussions as well, it is overwhelmingly more present in paleontology. This is because neontologists often have more distinct means by which they delineate species. The Biological Species Concept (BSC), for instance, separates different species based on whether they can mate and produce fertile, viable offspring (Mayr (1987)). Just as it is with the BSC, many neontological definitions are reliant on observing behavior and insights into physiology or genetics. Paleontologists, on the other hand, do not have such detailed information. All we have to work with are the fragmented and otherwise incomplete skeletons of a few individuals from what would have been large populations of ancient species. These fossils do not just represent a fragment of the variation present in such populations but, more aptly, a fragment of a fragment of this variation. Paleontologists are thus forced to rely solely on incomplete morphology in our attempt to separate closely related fossil populations.

With such limited information, many pursue species diagnosis by looking for traits that could identify particular adaptive zones (Simpson (1953)). Adaptive zones represent the ecological and behavior niche filled by a species which influence the evolution of characteristics needed to navigate it. Neanderthals, for instance, are adapted to highly carnivorous diets in the Ice Age landscape of Middle Pleistocene Europe. They are thus distinguished from other species through the presence of adaptive features specific to such conditions. By this model, any Middle Pleistocene hominin that is adapted to cold environments (increased robusticity, wide nasal aperture, highly carnivorous diet) would be filtered into the “Neanderthal box”. While this makes sense, it is important to keep in mind that similar selective pressures can operate simultaneously on different taxa (especially if the taxa share a common ancestor). These definitions therefore can suffer from their generality, having a tendency to include a wide range of morphologically flexible traits that might suit ephemeral environmental trends. That is to say, they could very well include individuals from different taxa which happened to adapt similarly to changing environments (via convergent or parallel evolution). In this way, lumpers run the risk of creating polyphyletic groupings that are inconsistent with the evolutionary history of the taxon under investigation (look back at the shared trait caveat for a refresh on polyphyletic groupings). It is because of this that other paleontologists define species more specifically.

Instead of looking for broad trends, splitters tend to identify any distinction in morphology as qualifying criteria for species delineation. More akin to what are called Phenetic (and/or cladistic) species concepts, these researchers recognize distinct patterns in a population as the result of isolation (reproductive) and therefore as potentially unique evolutionary paths (Smith (2009); Sneath (1973)). The concept has strong footing in evolutionary theory but suffers from overlooking the preponderance of regional variation in known species. A phenetic researcher, for instance, might (often does) observe the differences in coat color and pattern observed in giraffe populations across Africa as qualifying criteria for species-level distinction (Figure 3). Each morphological variant clearly represents some degree of genetic isolation (Fennessy et al. (2016)) and therefore could be considered a separate evolutionary line, but can you imagine if we divided modern human populations in this way? No thank you.

The question at the base of the argument is when and where in an evolutionary lineage do we draw the dividing line? de Queiroz (2007) expertly discusses and illustrates the issue (Figure 2) showing what he calls the “Grey Zone” in which different researchers will ascribe one or two (sometimes more) species to the same population. It is perhaps because of this philosophical stalemate that many paleoanthropologists choose to sidestep the discussion altogether by employing generalized categories outside of the confines of evolutionary systematics . These categories are used to discuss populations expected to be related to each other (but in an indeterminate way) and share a distinct adaptive zone. It is largely a lumper philosophy but without arguing about which populations represent specific species or genera. When applied in this way, discussions of fossil populations reflect taxonomic Grades rather than evolutionary Clades .

Grades vs. Clades

Identifying the earliest members of this genus is somewhat tricky. The boundaries between taxa are always a little hazy, which is understandable, but it makes accurately teaching about the human fossil record difficult. That being said, I am somewhat pigheaded and masochistic and so feel obligated to represent this topic as accurately as possible. Easy answers seldom reflect reality and so I will illustrate some of the “dirty laundry” of the field. I know that this is an intro class and that I am supposed to simply tell you all that Ardipithecus evolved into Australopithecus and Au. africanus evolved into Homo habilis (as depicted in the textbook), but it really isn’t that straightforward. These episodes of speciation, whether anagenic or cladogenic, are observed in the fossil record through species with mosaic traits . As such, we often come across proverbial “grey areas” in which taxonomic assignment is particularly difficult.

It might be worthwhile having a short discussion regarding grades and clades. Some of these informal categories that we use are necessary but distinctly not distinct. Gracile and Robust australopithecines, for instance, are not formal taxonomic categories (though the designation of Paranthropus for the latter is certainly an attempt to formalize). These categories often arise when the research community has too little information, or is otherwise too divided on how to utilize the information at hand, to agree on a formal group. Hence the issue of gracile australopithecines from last week. This category is based on relative assessment of morphology and behavior. Gracile australopithecines can be described as the opposite of robust australopithecines in masticatory and/or body size, or it can be described as a specific evolutionary trend with unique adaptations and shifting behavior. In either case, the definition becomes operationally difficult as it lacks specific criteria in which fossils could be tested against. Such categories, however, are useful in explaining the general suite of adaptations. The dialog becomes, “while we don’t know exactly how to categorize these hominins, we do know that those species deemed to belong to this group share a common suite of behaviors and physical adaptations”.

The distinction we are making is known in taxonomy as that between Grades and Clades (Figure 4). This is something that you came across in our week 2 textbook reading (Ch. 5: Ways of Organizing Taxa). While establishing a distinct evolutionary clade is the ultimate goal of any taxonomic assessment, it is simply not always possible. The fossil record is fragmentary, sparse, and often ambiguous. In times past it was common practice to attribute a species name (including the genus) to any and all hominin fossils found. Regardless of the amount of descriptive morphology available. As we move away from this practice, more researchers are making use of gradistic descriptions until the more formal taxonomic issues are agreed upon and resolved. One of the most pertinent examples of this impasse in paleoanthropology centers on when, where, and how we define the earliest members of the genus Homo.

Lumper/Splitter Debates

Australopithecus vs. H. habilis

Defining our genus has been a contested endeavor since the time of Linnaeus, Darwin, and Huxley. Historically, the taxonomic world could mostly only agree on the singular extant species (being H. sapiens). More recently there has been a broad acceptance of H. neanderthalensis and H. erectus as well, but most of the other 10 plus Homo species are in constant flux. While many of the debates center on whether particular fossils should be incorporated into the aforementioned accepted Homo species or represent something new, a foundational taxonomic issue concerns how we partition fossils at the genus level. This is discussed a bit in Chapter 9 of your textbook

A distinct facet of this disagreement is the matter of Early Homo looking and behaving much the same as gracile australopithecines but illustrating landmark behaviors of Homo. This, again, illustrates the mosaic nature of adaptive shifts.

There exist two primary camps in this argument: those who believe Homo habilis and Homo rudolfensis represent the earliest members of our genus, and those that believe them to represent the latest members of Australopithecus. Consider Figure 5, and notice how Au. africanus overlaps with H. habilis. This not only underscores the coexistence of the two species but shows that they occupy the same morphological, behavioral, and ecological space (though not explicitly said in the graphic). H. habilis is barely distinguishable from Au. africanus. There is a slight increase in encephalization, somewhat smaller teeth, and, importantly, an association with a distinct stone tool industry. Why the distinction then? Mostly because of the last point. The clear patterns of a stone tool industry (see below) do not exist in the human fossil record until this moment. While there is evidence of opportunistic tool use and even rudimentary stone fracturing earlier in the record, there is no patterned industry that we can describe and define until we see H. habilis (see Lomekwian tools below). This characteristic has become a defining quality for the genus Homo, but it is not morphological. It does not confer direct physical traits that we can associate with human fossils. So what are we supposed to do? The most widely accepted approach is to identify a gradient of evolutionary change that transitions from Gracile Australopithecines to Early Homo. This gradient creates a grey area incorporating fossils which exhibit both primitive and derived traits. It draws a line between the genera by using the emergence of the Oldowan Tool Industry and suggests that the larger suite of characteristics that define our genus morphologically primary develop at the time of H. erectus (which is not too long after this).

Herein lies the trouble. A morphological grey area is generally not accepted in the Cladistic splitter mindset. The goal for a splitter is to develop a clean monophyletic group which has a single common ancestor and accounts for all descendent populations. Morphology is the diagnostic king and is an all or nothing qualifier. They identify every unique set of traits as representative of distinct taxa, which leads to each designated group being highly specific. This applies too to the genus level groupings. There is no room for the inclusion of species which have some but not all of the traits. This will come up again when we discuss “Archaic Homo sapiens”.

Proponents of this approach rightly illustrate that H. habilis more closely resembles gracile australopithecines than it does later species in the genus (i.e., H. erectus). The fossils, they suggest, should therefore not be included in Homo as they do not exhibit all of the inherited traits of the group. Sure enough, H. habilis does represent some of the traits better known in the genus (increased encephalization, reduced craniofacial robusticity, increased frontal lobe development), but is more indicative of a transitional species with a mosaic of Australopithecus and Homo traits (Aiello and Wheeler (1995); Antón et al. (2014); Kimbel (2009); Zanolli et al. (2026)). As noted, grey areas are generally not welcome in this philosophy. Lumpers, on the other hand, are less concerned with the specific morphology as they are with the evolutionary pattern illustrated by the fossil species (Aiello and Wheeler (1995); Zanolli et al. (2026)). Opposing the splitter perspective, these researchers argue that Early Homo illustrates a shift into the adaptive zone uniquely occupied by Homo. While the morphology of these hominins might be lacking some hallmark traits of later species, they illustrate trending adaptations which anticipate the full suite of characters to come. Thus, according to the lumpers in this argument, H. habilis represents the new adaptive zone of Homo and therefore belongs within that grouping.

As stated earlier, their cannot be a right or wrong answer here. The decision needs to be made based on what is most informative in our understanding of human evolution. Is it more instructive to think about early Homo as part of the Australopithecus clade due to shared morphology? Or is it more instructive to think about this species as part of the genus Homo becuase of the shift in adaptive strategy? I can see the value of either based solely on this arguument. There is one additional addendum to the splitter approach, however, that makes this philosophy difficult to reconcile. I mentioned that splitters do not like grey areas and that they strive to create monophyletic groupings, right? This specifically means that one genus, as a clade, cannot evolve into another genus. By their logic, Australopithecus could not have evolved into the genus Homo. They instead would be sister-taxa (they split into two groups from a single common ancestor).

Check out Figure 6. H. habilis is split from the genus Homo and added as a terminal branch on the Australopithecus lineage (Davies et al. (2024); Wood and Collard (1999a); Wood and Collard (1999b)). I suppose this could be a fine structure if 1) the accumulation of traits in Australopithecus did not anticipate the next stage of our evolution seen in the genus Homo and 2) if we had any fossils of Homo that coincided with species such as Au. anamensis or Au. afarensis. We don’t have any Homo fossils that date far enough back to support the single common ancestor model. Finally, I find it funny that this is a splitter argument given that the outcome would be to wildly broaden the adaptive zone (therefore lumping) of the Australopithecus genus.

H. habilis vs. H. rudolfensis

The debates get much simpler after the previous overview. This is in part because they fall back on questions of how much variation to allow and in part because aspects of the previous argument are really kinda weird (i.e., the aversion to transitional morphologies). Further, the arguements regarding species assignments within the genus Homo are far better at representing the divide between lumpers and splitters. In each case, the distinction lies in whether we account for regional variation within a single species or if each variant should represent a species on its own.

The divide between the earliest of Homo species (H. habilis and H. rudolfensis) is tricky. They both appear in the fossil record around the same time (~2.1-1.8 Ma) and are found in the same region (East Africa). While it can be argued that H. habilis occurs before H. rudolfensis, current research struggles to appropriately sort different fossils across these two taxa (Antón et al. (2014); Kimbel (2009); Spoor et al. (2015); Zanolli et al. (2026)) and so there is some difficulty in sorting the species into early and late occurrences. Despite this, research on the pooled sample of Early Homo fossils (including both proposed species) suggest that, despite the scant fossil material, the variation observed falls well-outside the expected range for a singular species (Villmoare (2018); Davies et al. (2024); Lieberman et al. (1996)). In considering the crania associated with each (Figure 7) some authors have suggested that the distinction is largely a reflection of sexual dimorphism or indicative of progressive anagenic change on the way to H. erectus. Not surprisingly, the arguements present strong and valid evidence on both sides of the field. For now, while the current paradigm is leaning more strongly towards a two-species diagnosis, the problem of sparse and fragmentary fossils remains a distinct hurdle (only one decent H. rudolfensis skull). Until we find more fossils from this time period, keeping a conservative view of Early Homo (one species) will continue to be the consensus in the textbooks. This is perhaps one of the greatest strengths of the lumper philosophy, they do not make rash diagnoses. The approach emphasizes that we do not know what the species looked like at the population level until we have a population-level sample size (more than a couple of fragmentary fossils).

H. erectus vs. H. ergaster

Homo erectus is major species of interest in paleoanthropology. It illustrates the first species to develop modern human limb proportions (making it a far more proficient biped), lose most of the ape-like body hair while simultaneously evolving a high concentration of sweat pores and dark skin for enhanced thermoregulation (see super cool research on skin color below), become endurance hunters, and to control and use fire (for cooking, warmth, protection, et cetera). It is attributed with increased cognitive abilities as illustrated by the learned mental template for the Acheulean Handaxe and inferred cooperative and systematic hunting as well as through its navigation of environments leading to a more global distribution (likely the first to venture outside of the African continent). The significance of H. erectus, therefore, cannot be overstated.

The global distribution of H. erectus is both fascinating and problematic for discussions of taxonomy. As with other “cosmopolitan” species which exhibit global ranges, there are distinct differences between far separated populations. Of particular note are the distinctions between East African and East Asian assemblages (also between East Asian and SE Asian populations, to a lesser degree). Especially when the respective fossil populations were first discovered, researchers perceived a large morphological gap that would, in many other circumstances, justify the separate species designations. Thus, H. erectus was ascribed to the fossils found throughout East and SE Asia (first named by Eugene DuBois) and H. ergaster was attributed to the East African fossils. These were represented mostly by specimens such as ER 3733 in East Africa and NG 13 from SE Asia (Figure 8). As more fossils became known, however, the degree of difference between the two regions became less stark. East Asian morphotypes began to appear in East Africa (e.g., OH 9) and fossils found in West Asia (Dminisi, Georgia) appeared to represent both forms.

While dedicated splitters still commit to differentiating the populations, a vast majority of paleoanthropologists recognize the divide one based on inconsequential traits and differences in size. With a greater abundance of fossil material than that related to the prior debate (H. habilis vs H. rudolfensis), detailed analyses of H. erectus variation has benefitted from a decently large cranial sample. Such efforts illustrate that the differences between regional populations of this species amount to less variation than is seen in modern human populations from different global regions (Antón (2003); Baab (2008); Baab (2016); Rightmire (1998)).

The Problem with Archaic H. sapiens

Your reading for this week covered two chapters written by very different anthropologists. While I generally like the approach of Chapter 10 on Early Homo, I have some issues with Chapter 11. Specifically, the decision to use the term “Archaic Homo sapiens” is highly problematic. While I often like gradistic descriptors to carry on a conversation (like we often do for Early Homo), this group is notably muddled and misleading (Bae et al. (2023); Roksandic et al. (2022b); Roksandic et al. (2022a)). Without making a big argument about the existence and placement of fossil species such as H. heidelbergensis (of which is highly contentious), there is one strong issue at hand that you should all be able to grasp at this point. Implementing an “Archaic Homo sapiens” grade suggests that every fossil population encompassed by the group is directly on the evolutionary line to Homo sapiens. This is blatantly not the case with many of the fossils mentioned under this tag in the textbook.

The low hanging fruit in this discussion is the authors’ decision to include Neanderthals in the grade. If you haven’t realized it yet, Neanderthals did not evolve into H. sapiens. These populations are sister-taxa which diverged from a common ancestor. Calling H. neanderthalensis an Archaic H. sapiens directly contradicts our current understanding of the human fossil record. This is likely to be true also for H. heidelbergensis (which is itself roughly synonymous with H. rhodesiensis and H. bodoensis) but there are more pressing taxonomic questions regarding the division of this particular group.

The takeaway here is that, if we are going to employ a grade such as Archaic H. sapiens, the fossils attributed to this grade must at least be thought to be on the H. sapiens line. Plain and simple.

Stone Tools

When investigating tool use in the human fossil record, two points of contention often arise. The first of these involves how we identify the earliest evidence of tool use. This is more specifically directed at when we can first see the application of stone tools to animal butchery and, therefore, the advance of carnivory (shift in adaptive zone). Claims of “cut marked” animal bones from sites such as Dikika and Bouri in Ethiopia dominate this discussion as they both predate the earliest evidence of manufactured tools. The second area of interest, then, is how we identify the earliest stone tool industries. We will come to see that these industries are distinct in paleoanthropology. They relate something very much different than the opportunistic use of tools in non-human primates and preclude the rudimentary fragmented stones that sometimes appear from such expediency (see Lomekwian tools below).

While it may seem like these two issues are necessarily bound together, they appear differently in the fossil record and have influenced different perspectives on when, where, and by whom tool using cultures were first developed. The next couple of sections discuss some of the debates regarding stone tools and evidence of animal butchery. Each of the points here were mentioned briefly in the text but deserve a bit of explanation.

Indirect Evidence of Tool Use (Taphonomy)

Difficulties arise when attempting to answer questions concerning the behavior of our human ancestors. Since behavior itself does not fossilize, anthropologists look to the field of taphonomy to discern discrete traces of a wide variety of past activities. While not an exact science, this research has become a necessary component of osteological analysis in zooarchaeology, forensic anthropology, and human origins studies. Taphonomic investigation has been incorporated into paleontological research since the early 19th century, centering on how we interpret post-mortem processes that alter organic material. Those who focus in this research investigate all possible agents that have acted on a once living organism (typically vertebrate) from the moment it died to the moment it was recovered in survey or excavation.

The most common application of taphonomic analysis in anthropology concerns the reconstruction of human subsistence patterns. Often situated in the narrower field of zooarchaeology, an inspection of faunal elements may lead researchers to answer questions of early human diet, animal procurement strategies, and predator-prey relations (Lyman (1994)). Broadly, these investigations seek to determine if the materials found were initially deposited due to natural processes or as a result of human activity. Bone modifications resulting from human behavior typically occur during the animal butchery process (the reduction of carcasses into consumable parts) (Lyman (1987); White (1992)). This process includes the skinning, dismembering, and defleshing of an animal, as well as the extraction of marrow and brain tissue via bone fracture (White (1992)). All of the aforementioned actions may leave diagnostic taphonomic signatures on bone and can be observed as such. The identification of these signatures is one of the most widely used methods employed when discussing ancient human behavior and are typically presented as bone scaring and/or fracturing. Such lines of evidence, however, are difficult to interpret and even more difficult to prove as diagnostic of a specific behavior (Binford (1981)).

Any number of African Plio-Pleistocene fossil sites could provide a convincing case for hominin presence. While researchers are often careful to limit such interpretations to sites containing associated hominin fossils or artifacts, recent years have shown resurgence in a reliance on indirect evidence. This was highlighted by the 2010 claim for humanly modified bones occurring across 3.39 million year old sediments in Ethiopia’s Lower Awash Valley. Recovered by the Dikika Research Project (DRP), the fossilized faunal elements, incised with long fine striations, predate previously recognized butchery marks by nearly 800,000 years (McPherron et al. (2010); McPherron et al. (2011)). While this temporal difference initially draws skepticism, the idea of tool use in australopithecines is not entirely outlandish (see below). The taphonomic evidence presented by the DRP, however, was minimal and unsubstantiated when considered against other known modifying agents (i.e., carnivore teeth marks). After having identified the abrasions as “high confidence stone-tool cut marks”, critics were quick to question the contextual, anatomical and morphological properties of the found fossils (McPherron et al. (2010); Njau (2012)).

Subsequent research of the Dikika remains illustrated that, rather than human induced cutmarks, the bones were marked by the abrasions left by crocodile teeth (Njau (2012)). The Dikika taphonomic signatures have therefore been largely dismissed as evidence of butchery. While this does not prove that hominins were incapable of butchering animals and consuming animal protein, it does mean that evidence of this process without the direct association of stone tools is a tenuous process. Even the more widely accepted taphonomic cases in paleoanthropology (such as Bouri) are highly scrutinized and more commonly being dismissed with new analytical advances (Sahle et al. (2017)). Thus, the evidence of tool use and carnivory prior to the appearance of the Oldowan Tool Industry (and H. habilis) is yet insufficient to illustrate the adaptive shift in human evolution.

Indirect Evidence of Tool Use (non-human analogs)

Taphonomy aside, we should expect that australopithecines were tool users. This premise is well-accepted in our field with an underlying knowledge that behavioral changes tend to precede anatomical changes. This may sound somewhat Lamarckian but, on an evolutionary timeline, it’s true. Thus, hominins are likely to have been using stones as tools and even producing stone tools of a sort long before we have a strong archaeological record of it. While there is little question that tool industries were first propagated by members of our genus, using stones as tools would not have been a novel behavior. Observational and experimental studies with chimpanzees, for instance, suggest that the earliest origins of tool manufacture may not have been such a great cognitive leap.

Chimpanzees, having lesser relative brain size than Au. africanus and comparable brain size to Au. afarensis, are well-established tool users (Panger et al. (2002)). Specifically, the nut-harvesting chimpanzees of West Africa (Ivory Coast, Guinea, and Liberia) set a good model for early stone tool using capacities in the hominin line. When first documented in 1844, chimpanzees were said to crack nuts in the same manner as humans. As interest in this activity grew, more detailed investigations depicted the highly intellectual and even cultural nature of chimpanzee tool use. Behaviors which factored into this included preferential selection and transport of materials, mental mapping of resource areas, and transmission of tool using behavior to younger generations.

Christophe and Hedwige Boesch began reporting more thorough accounts of chimpanzees using “natural hammers” in 1981 (Boesch and Boesch (1982); Boesch and Boesch (1990)). Their work was performed along the N’Zo-Sassandra River in Taï National Park, Ivory Coast, beginning in 1979 and lasted for three years. The chimpanzees in this region processed nuts seasonally, from February to August, and acquired an estimated 3000 calories a day from it (Mercader et al. (2007)). The highest value, and most sought after nut in this region grows from the Panda oleosa tree. Having too hard a shell to crack with a wooden club, chimps seek out stones of granite and laterite to use as tools. Given the relative scarcity of stones, especially granite, the materials were presumed to have been selected and transported from off-site outcrops as far as 500 meters away. Stones were then used excessively for long periods of time (according to use wear) and carried with individuals across different sites (Boesch and Boesch (1982)). During their research the authors noted higher selection for lighter, though adequately hard, hammers when moving to trees farther away. Not being able to see many of these trees at the time of tool selection, it was presumed that they chose stones based on their knowledge of the distance between sites and the costs of carrying a weighted tool.

The importance of Panda nuts, and the tools needed to extract them, is further highlighted in these studies by the transmission of nut-cracking strategies to younger troop members. Taught in three stages, young chimpanzees begin to learn to proficiently crack nuts by the time they are three years old. Mothers first leave stones and nuts at an anvil site to stimulate interest in their young. To facilitate the action they allow the then curious infant to take resources from them during harvest. Finally, if the mother does not think the juvenile is properly using the tool, she will actively teach them to do so (Boesch et al. (1994)). These behaviors were identified in a single troop on four separate occasions, leading the authors to consider stone tool use as culturally unique to the chimpanzees along the N’Zo-Sassandra River in Taï Park (Boesch et al. (1994)). The behaviors exhibited by the Taï Park chimpanzees reflect multiple levels of complex thought beyond that of opportunistic tool use. They portray an understanding of material structure and durability, distribution of resource sites within their environment, and the significance of teaching this behavior to their young.

When such observations are related to discussions of hominin tool use, it is likely safe to assume that the australopiths had the same cognitive capacity for this behavior as modern Great Apes. Problems arise, however, when correlating modern behavioral studies with archaeological populations. To do so appropriately, it becomes necessary to match the material aftermath of chimpanzee tool sites with archaeological sites. Though few in number when compared with hominin tool assemblages, a handful of “chimpanzee stone age” (CSA) sites have been identified in West Africa (Mercader et al. (2007)). The sites date to roughly 4,300 BP and are isolated from nearby human cultural areas. Among the scatter of nutshells and anvils, researchers at the site of Panda 1 identified 4,500 grams of fractured stone. Interpreted as the unintentional result of continual percussive actions, the found debitage is said to resemble some earlier Oldowan artifacts. Overall, however, the materials were suggested as too simple to be readily identified as the consequence of tool using without other associated artifacts (Mercader et al. (2007)). While too ambiguous to be archaeologically visible on their own, these debitage scatters do provide insight into how flaked tools may have first occurred.

This draws up an important point when discussing tool-making as a distinct hominin behavior. The importance placed on this behavioral shift is not associated with the use of tools, the selectivitity of materials, or even the production and use of broken stone implements. The evolutionary significant phenomenon that is under investigation is the development of Stone Tool Industries. This is of particular importance when comparing the supposed “Lomekwian” tools with the later Oldowan Tool Complex, as we will see below.

Stone Tool Industries

Using Tool Industry as a category is not far off from our general approach to taxonomy. There should be a degree of discerable morphology and an associated behavior that qualifies the material to fit into distinct categories. Much like the fossils we are studying, the larger the sample size the more confidence we have in the classification, with those materials falling outside of the established range of variation being subject to criticism and exhausting debate.

Tool industries benefit from being recognizable and describable. Even the simplest industry, the Oldowan Tool Complex, has unique identifiable qualities. Though they may look very much like randomly broken rocks when seen on their own, when seen together there appears a clear pattern of development. The uniformity in design conveys a few important insights to us when considering human evolution. First, it suggests that the creation of these tools is intentional (not always easy to determine). Second, it suggests the tool making was planned (materials were chosen for this purpose). Finally, and very important to the discussion, this uniformity through time and space suggests that the process of tool making was taught. These three aspects of tool manufacture are similar to what was seen with the chimpanzee example above, except that the intent here is not just to use a stone as a tool but to fashion a stone into a better tool. And so we get to the problem at hand, are our earliest examples of stone tools in the human fossil record a reflection of stones being used as tools or as stones being manufactured into tools? This question has been pretty-well put to bed for the Oldowan Tool Industry (clear, intentional manufacture) but is still highly relevant to the recently discovered Lomewian tools.

Lomekwian

This topic is still very much alive in paleoanthropological debate and one that I am not super familiar with, so bear with me.

Until 2015 the earliest recognized stone tools represented the 2.5 Ma Oldowan Industry first defined by Louis Leaky in mid-20th century. As described in your text, these tools are simple but distinct in their pattern of manufacture and predictable outcome (typically in the form of an Oldowan Chopper). These tools were likely developed to better facilitate the somewhat simple task of breaking bones for the extraction of bone marrow. For this purpose, the primary modification of a stone <span title=“primary stone implement” core , involved hitting it with a large hammerstone in an effort to remove a flake . This created the Oldowan Chopper which was, for all intents and purposes, a handheld rock with one sharp side. These hominin tool makers could then use the implement to chop at long bones that would break open and expose the nutrient rich marrow. Thus we define the Oldowan Tool Industry.

The 2015 publication on the 3.3 Ma Lomekwian tools, however, introduced new discussions of how such industries should be defined (Harmand et al. (2015); Lewis and Harmand (2016)). These very rudimentary tools consisted of cores, flakes, hammerstones, and anvils. The discoverers claim that the materials were then used for processing meat (therefore illustrating the adaptive shift) but research on the site and the tools themselves do not quite agree. While their are animal bones found at the Lomekwi site, none of these illustrate the striations that would be expected from butchery (see taphonomy discussion above). Heck, they don’t even show carnivore teeth marks. So if the tool makers were processing meat, it would have been done somewhere away from the manufacturing site. Many authors reference the taphonomic material from Dikika as potential support for the argument, but the Dikika material has already been successfully disproven. Further, paeloarchaeologists have methods to determine what stone tools were used for by examining the microscopic signatures left on their cutting surfaces. As of yet, none of the materials seem to have been used for animal carcass processing (Plummer et al. (2025)).

It seems that Lomekwian tools were unlikely used for the adaptive shift associated with hominin carnivory. That said, the appearance of these tools could illustrate something new in hominin behavior at this time. Given the co-occurence of these implements with Au. afarensis, if they were to reflect a pattern distinct from what is seen in non-human primates, they would still show an advance in behavior along our evolutionary line. The verdict on whether or not this is the case is still out. Many researchers pursuing the matter are quick to point out that the process of making Lomekwian tools is at a distinctly higher cognitive level than that seen from Capuchin monkeys (see Figure 14), but the divide between Lomekwian and CSA material is far less clear. Lombard et al. (2019), for instance, illustrates that intentional flake production would have required a more complex cognitive process for the hominin tool makers than chimpanzees, but there is currently little evidence suggesting that the production of flakes is the intended outcome. Otherwise, the passive hammer knapping and bipolar flake production are remarkebly similar to the Panda nut processors from West Africa. This is not the case, however, with the production of Oldowan tools which were constructed through hand-held techniques (holding the core in one hand and hammerstone in the other during production) and, to some degree, illustrate the intentional resharpining (retouching) of flakes for continued use (Mesfin et al. (2026); Plummer et al. (2025)).

In sum i’ll stress again that the arguments surrounding Lomekwain tools are something that I am less familiar with (never been much of an archaeologist). My first impression was that they illustrated little difference from the sort of debitage seen in other cases of expedient tool manufacture and/or unintentional tool shaping. That being said, some of the Oldowan materials I have seen are not too far off from this. What I can say with some confidence is that these “tools” so far do not illustrate the same sort of adaptative shift as later tool indutries and are therefore less significant to our general discussion of major shifts in human evolution.
