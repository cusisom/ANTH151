Chapter Summary

We are finishing up this unit with an overview of the Peopling of the Pacific (including Polynesia). As in our previous discussion, we will consider the corridors and barriers that influenced ancestral populations eastward from West Asia, how some of them came to populate South China and Taiwan, and how these communities ventured out into the largest body of water on the planet. Again, global climate fluctuations effected the state of these corridors and barriers, facilitating or hindering population movements almost like a traffic light. It is commonly accepted that this migration occured on a Southern Route out of Africa, following coastlines and river valleys of the Arabian Peninsula and South Asia (Pakistan, India, and Bangladesh) before making into Southeast Asia. This of course wasn’t the only wave of migrants that made it into Asia in the Late Pleistocene (see previous lecture), but it is the one from which we see the rise of the Austronesian language group. This chapter aims to review the evidence for this migration (archaeological, genetic, and linguistic) and illustrate different interpretations of how it happened.

The Austronesian Language Group

Our primary focus will be on a migration known as the Austronesian Expansion. The term Austronesia refers to a language group which had an early origin and subsequently diversified into one of the largest language groups in the world. Thus, while we will still need to go deeper back in time to sort out the southern migration Out-of-Africa, it seems pertinenet to first introduce the Austronesian populations and the insight linguistics provides for their origins and Pacific expansion.

Prior to about AD 1500, the Austronesian language family represented the most widely dispersed group of languages in the world. From Paiwan, Tagalog, and Malay in the West to Fijian, Samoan, and ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi in the East, this linguistic family currently comprises up to 1,200 distinct languages throughout Island Southeast Asia (ISEA) and Oceania (Klamer (2019); Stephen Lansing et al. (2011)). While rooted in South China and Southeast Asia, the Proto-Austronesian language became fully fledged as Austronesian in Taiwan as Neolithic cultures began to take hold across the island. As the cultures continued to develop so did the languages. Over time they diversified into a variety of dialects and eventually established descendant languages across different parts of the region. It is this diversification that provides us with some of the earliest insight into the spread of people across the islands of the Pacific.

Given this linguistic diversity and its clues to the Peopling of the Pacific, it is useful to think of languages in the same way that we do fossil species (i.e., through phylogeny). This will come into play a little later as well when we discuss the Austronesian genetic phylogenies, so it is worth wrapping your head around now. Recall that phylogenetics tracks the development of a lineage from origin, through its evolution and dispersal. As is the case with biological evolution, languages can be traced to ancestral groups which changed through time and diversified as speakers of that language splintered from the origin (often designated as a Proto- version of that language). The Austronesian languages have mostly been understood in this way. While a large linguistic family with many variations, linguists have identified ten primary branches, nine of which are exclusive to the island of Taiwan and one that has spread throughout Oceania.

Lingusitic Attributes

Identifying the region of primary linguistic diversity is a key approach to determining where and when a language first developed. Observing that nine of the ten Austronesian branches exist only in Taiwan, for instance, is a good indicator that this island represents the origin point. At this origin, the Austronesian settler populations grew and spread across the island, changing aspects of the language along the way. The earliest expansions away from the common origin are illustrated in the degree of language diversity near its center (first generation descendants). As these communities disperse farther in time and space, however, they only bring with them a small portion of the original linguistic variation. Thus, as cultures get farther from their linguistic origin, the languages become less diverse.

To track this geographic expansion, think of it like a massive, generational game of telephone. When a small group leaves the focal point, they carry only one specific version of the original message with them. As their descendants move from island to island, minor changes in pronunciation and vocabulary accumulate with each step. By the time the message reaches the far edges of the expansion, it sounds entirely different from the source. Because these changes happen in predictable patterns, linguists can trace these altered versions back to the original homeland by identifying what are known as cognates (words that sound similar and have similar meanings across different languages).

An example of this can be found in the ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi word for a canoe or boat. While the ʻŌlelo word is waʻa, this same structure is referred to as waka in Aotearoa (New Zealand), vaʻa in Samoa, and wangga (with the gg pronounced as a single syllable) in Fiji (Pawley and Pawley (1994)). Moving westward into Island Southeast Asia, the term shifts to bangka in Tagalog and wangkang in Malay. To bridge the final gap back to the Taiwan focal point, linguists track these variants to the ancient Proto-Austronesian root qabaN, referring to a dugout canoe. This root influence is still retained in Taiwan as qavan in the Thao language and avang in Siraya. If this is something that anybody has interest in exploring, check out the interactive Austronesian Comparative Dictionary webpage from Robert Blust (The Austronesian Comparative Dictionary (ACD) Online). There are plenty of examples that illustrate these linguistic relationships. We happened to use the word for boat/canoe due to its relevance to our discussion.

It is through this branch of historical linguistic research that the first inklings of a Taiwan origin for Austronesian and Polynesian cultures came about. Languages are fluid, however, and highly susceptible to changes due to cultural shifts, back-migrations, and historical intrusions. Thus, while providing strong evidence of this connection, additional lines of evidence are needed to trace this narrative to specific, physical population movements. Furthermore, if our goal is to connect this to the much earlier Paleolithic migrations out of Africa, we will need to look for a more permanent, biological record of inheritance.

Origins and Migrations

As we did in the previous chapter, we are going to begin this discussion of migrations at the time of the last major Out-of-Africa pulse around 71,500 years ago (Oppenheimer (2012)). Instead of following these populations into Northeast Asia, however, we will concentrate on movements along the coasts and rivers of South and into Southeast Asia.

One thing we didn’t discuss in the last chapter was the issue of preservation and the likely absence of data due to environmental shifts. We did review the rise and fall of sea levels during Glacial and Interglacial periods and their effects on migratory corridors in the past but we didn’t consider how these could also impact the visibility of the archaeological record. Regarding the Kelp Highway hypothesis (last chapter), for instance, one problem faced by archaeologists is that the migrations would have occurred during the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) when sea levels were much lower than they are today. It stands to reason that many of the small population centers or encampments that would have existed along the way would be near the shores of this coastline. With the climate reversal into MIS 1 (current era), the sea levels have risen considerably and submerged many of these lowland coastal landscapes (Erlandson and Moss (2008)). This same situation is true for coasts along the Southern Route out of Africa, making much of the archaeological signature invisible (Oppenheimer (2012)).

In addition to submerged landscapes, the Southern Route suffers from warm, moist, and often tropical regions along the Gulf of Aden, Gulf of Oman, Arabian Sea, and Bay of Bengal. While I love the tropics, they are terrible for preservation. Just about anything organic (e.g., wood, textiles, bodies) is likely to rapidly decompose in these conditions. The process of decomposition (decomp), where materials breakdown into simpler products (also referred to as decay or rot) is generally dependent on three conditions: Heat, Moisture, and Oxygen. Limiting any one of these conditions will greatly slow the decomp process, while limiting two or all of these conditions can stop the process entirely (which is why we often find preserved remains/materials in freezing, arid, and/or anerobic conditions). Of course, the converse is also true. Increasing the intensity of any of these conditions can also have the effect of accelerating decomp. Herein lies the problem. Many of the environments along the Southern Coast, even in colder global eras, remained tropical (increasing heat and moisture) (Figure 3). Thus, along with the likely submerged archaological sites, many of the materials that escaped the rising sea levels were rapidly decomposed by tropical conditions. Especially over the past 14,000 years or so of our current Interglacial. In part because of this, for the discussion of the Southern dispersal out of Africa and into Southeast Asia and the Pacific, we will rely primarily on genetic evidence.

Genetic Evidence

Discussing genetic lineages can be a bit challenging in a class like this, so we will keep it as simple as possible. When examining Haplogroups, we are using genetic data to establish lineages through the same processes of inheritance that we learned about in Chapter 1. Though it is often easier to think about physical traits being inherited than specific gene variants, the basics are the same. One version of a gene or another will be passed to the next generation. Haplogroups are generally formed from mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and Y chromosome (Y-DNA) lineages due to their complete inheritance (non-recombination), allowing for mostly untampered genetic legacies. Mutations, however, still have the power to alter the genes found along these chromosomes. While they can sometimes impact fitness, many of them have little to no effect on the individual, instead altering non-important (often non-coding) details of different genes. Despite this low impact, such gene variants have a tendency of sticking around and eventually give modern researchers something to track through time. From this, genetic phylogenies () are created to illustrate the inter-relatedness of populations. As certain gene variants become more prominent in different populations and regions, we can follow histories of mutation from ancestral forms. in the figure depicts the distribution of major mtDN (maternally inherited) haplogroups across the globe. This is a high value image. It confirms some of the ancestries we have been discussing throughout this unit. Notice how the L haplogroup is pretty exclusive to Africa but that it gives rise to both the M and the N haplogroups (from L3). This marks the L3-lineage Exodus (Chapter 10). You can also see how lineages descending from both N and M came together to contribute to North American ancestry (groups C and D from the larger M haplogroup and groups X, A, and B from the larger N haplogroup). While this is an overlay of the broader haplogroups, we can be much narrower to trace more finite population movements from minor divergences (, for instance).

Haplogroup Relatedness/Phylogeny

The papers cited in this section come together to tell a pretty nice story of population movements into and out of Southeast Asia, but they use distinctively different datasets. Though each is used to confirm the migration trajectory, some of the studies use Y-DNA data (paternally inherited) while others use mtDNA (maternally inherited). These tend to reference different haplogroups of a similar name. For instance, there are F Haplogroups found in both Y-DNA data and mtDNA data. For Y-DNA data, however, this F group acts as an ancient ancestral macrogroup (descended from CF) that eventually gives rise to the paternal N lineage, whereas it descends from the N macro-haplogroup ancestral population in mtDNA records (, Images 1 and 2). These nuances are not important for your understanding of this discussion in our class, but it is important to clarify. Just know that I am using both datasets for this discussion.

mtDNA Trajectory of Gene Flow

Oppenheimer (2012) follows the L3 expansion out of Africa at the MIS 5e/MIS 4 transition into South Asia and ultimately into East and South East Asia. They suggest that the rise of the N haplogroup populations from the L3 expansion occurred in South Asia at some point between 87-59 ka (though archaeological evidence favors an MIS 3 date range between approximately 57-50 ka). Following the mtDNA trail, Soares et al. (2011) identifies the descendant B haplogroup as emerging in Southern East Asia or Southeast Asia at about 44 ka. You can find this branching pattern (L3➜N➜R➜B) in Figure 4. According to these papers this inheritence pattern took roughly 10,000 years, during which time the human population in the region experienced oscillating periods of bottleneck and recovery. As such, the environment had an effect of pruning the genetic phylogeny through bottleneck events, and facilitating its diversification through founder effect events. This pattern of nested haplogroups can be a bit tedious but will ultimately provide a genetic map of population expansions (see figure below). The lineages continue to diversify and divide through time until we get to the specific Polynesian motif (B4a1a1a) that spreads throughout Remote Oceania. Y-DNA Trajectory of Gene Flow

While there is plenty of research following this same migratory path using Y-Chromosome gene data, we are mostly looking at this because of the discussion in the assigned papers (Gibbons (2001); Gibbons (2016)). As discussed above, prior to genetics both linguistic and archaeological studies indicated a Taiwan origin and rapid expansion through Near Oceania of Austronesian speaking people. These people developed the Lapita Cultural Complex, leaving traces of their cultural toolkit (pottery, crops, domesticated animals) at each island they colonized. In this process it was thought that the Lapita interacted with nearby populations in Melanesia (e.g., Papua New Guinea) through the Triple-I model, though the influence of genetic admixture on Polynesian ancestry was thought to be minimal. This was further supported as mtDNA studies illustrated the rise and spread of the Polynesian Motif (B4a1a1a) in Near Oceania. The pace and trajectory of the Austronesian Expansion was thus referred to colloquially as an “Express Train” moving through Near Oceania and out to Remote Oceania. Challenges to this model arose in the early 2000s, however, as Y-DNA research suggested a much more significant Melanesian (or Papuan) genetic contribution to the Polynesian gene pool.

The first major contribution to the “Slow Boat” model of Austronesian Expansion came from Kayser et al. (2000). Examining Y-DNA, they illustrated that a significant percentage of the paternally inherited Y-chromosomes in Polynesia are attributable to Papuan ancestry. This indicated that the expansion didn’t blow by Melanesia on an express train but rather settled into the region and interacted and intermixed with the indigenous island populations extensively. More specifically, it was thought that Papuan men would join Austronesian matrilocal communities. Thus, men would leave their familial communities and join the expanding Austronesian populations. As stated in the Gibbons (2001) article, this research pretty-well established a pattern of prolonged admixture between the two populations. This again changed, however, as genetics research advanced in its capability to examine the entire genome (not just the mtDNA and Y-DNA). While previous work provided some insight into maternal and paternal lineages, the analyses were inherently narrow (small windows into genetic legacies). The Skoglund et al. (2016) study using whole genome analysis provided revolutionary insight into the seemingly contradictory mtDNA/Y-DNA explanations.

The whole genome analysis illustrated a simple fact, that the contribution of Papuan ancestry into Polynesia was a recent event. This was not discovered because the original study was wrong about the preponderance of Papuan Y-DNA in these peoples, but rather because it was wrong about the timing. Skoglund et al. (2016) were able to investigate the genomes from archaological populations found in Vanuatu and Tonga which date back to the expanding Lapita culture (~2,680-2,340 years before present (BP)). Contrary to modern Polynesian populations, these individuals show exhibit no evidence of Papuan admixture, proving that the overwhelming presence of Papuan Y-DNA in modern populations came from a more recent migration (Spriggs et al. (2019)).

Summing Up

From the given evidence we can conclude with a few important points. First, the mtDNA L3 lineage represents the root of all non-African populations across the globe. Second, the L3 lineaage diversified into two primary branches (M and N) in South Asia during MIS 3. Along the N-Lineage line, populations moved East into Southeast Asia where they eventually established th B4 haplogroup. Geneticists then can identify the expansion of this population throughout ISEA and Near Oceania, ultimately illustrating movements across the whole of the Polynesian Triangle. While Y-DNA research in the 1990s confused this process a little, it has become clear through whole genome research that the Austronesian ancestors originated in Taiwan and intermixed relatively little with resident populations as they spread across the islands of Near Oceania (i.e., Papuans). Thus, genetic research has provided a direct line from the Polynesian islands back to the Proto-Austronesian populations which occupied East and Southeast Asia during the Neolithic.

Archaeology and the Lapita Cultural Complex

Perhaps one reason for the initial debate between “express train”, “entangled banks”, and “slow boat” proponents is the preponderance of archaeological sites throughout Near Oceania which long pre-date the Austronesian (Lapita) occupation of these islands. The first successful colonization of the Pacific, afterall, occured much earlier than the Austronesian expansion. Our previous discussion regarding the genetic trail from OOA to the island of Taiwan was tracking a deliberate trajectory. Knowing that Polynesian origins can be traced to these nested genetic lineages, I decided to stick with that particular line of inheritance. This, however, should not be interpreted as the only spread of people into ISEA and Near Oceania. Again, the route we discussed just so happens to be the one leading specifically to Polynesia. That aside, a much earlier intrusion to this region can be identified as early in time as MIS 4, approximately 65-50 ka (Clarkson et al. (2017); Shaw et al. (2022); Smith (1995); Summerhayes (2023)). The route taken by these earlier emigrants was largely provided by the much lower sea levels of MIS 4 and MIS 2 when the Malay Peninsula was joined with Indonesia (as far east as Bali) and mainland Southeast Asia. Regarded as the supercontinent Sunda, this landmass was separated from another (Sahul) comprised of Australia and New Guinea by relatively narrow oceanic gaps between the islands of Wallacea (Borreggine et al. (2026); Kealy et al. (2018); Kirch (2010)). While this route was not completely crossable on land, the gaps between the islands of Indonesia and Sahul would have been relatively small (with at least decent visibility between each) (Borreggine et al. (2026)).

Contention regarding the Austronesian-first or Melanesian-first scenarios of Polynesian colonization, when put into this framework, are understandable. For a long time the only evidence of Austronesian origins was linguistic. Languages, of course, can spread in a number of ways and cannot alone be used to trace ancestry (despite providing good evidence). Further, while the Lapita people of the Neolithic are seldom contested as the ancestors of modern Polynesians, there was no clear archaeological signature that these people came from Taiwan. Thus, proponents of the Melanesian-first model (or at very least the slow-boat with high-levels of cultural and genetic admixture) saw the long-established cultural groups in the region as the most likely to subsequently colonize the rest of the Pacific. And why not? It stands to reason that populations with a 40,000-year history in the region would continue to advance their maritime technology and push out into the open ocean. All this is to say that the historical debate was entirely reasonable. It just so happens that the overwhelming majority of modern archaeological, biomolecular, and ancient DNA evidence has since disproved that hypothesis. With this out of the way, let’s talk about why the Austronesians set out to explore and colonize the regions untouched (or only briefly occupied) by the long-established indigenous communities.

Lapita: Origins and Archaeological Signature

By now you already know that we have to leap ahead in our timeline to discuss the Austronesian expansion into the Pacific. According to Ko et al. (2014), Proto-Austronesian speakers made it to the Island of Taiwan only as early as about 6000 years ago. They then spread from North to South across the island with populations setting out to sea by approximately 4000 years ago. It is important to take a second and recognize the 2000 year gap between the settlement of Taiwan and the subsequent Austronesian expansion. Really though, think of how far we have come in 2000 years. This period was a major time of diversification, innovation, and cultural development. It was from this crucible that the later seafaring populations were able to conquer the Pacific.

I suppose this is a good time to introduce the people we have been discussing. Instead of simply referring to Austronesians (which include populations across Indonesia, Malaysia, and as far west as Madagascar), we can begin concentrating more specifically on an archaeological culture known as the Lapita. To clarify, they are regarded as an archaeological culture because many of their signature characteristics did not continue through time. Instead, their descendents developed new lifeways that distinguish them from their cultural forebearers. The Lapita are thus a distinctly recognizable culture that are identifiable through their archaeological signature. Among the material culture associated with Lapita are the remains of domesticated crops and animals, indicators of settlement structure and subsistence economy, and especially the technological and artistic advances associated with pottery production. These lines of evidence, when found together, are commonly referred to as the Lapita Package and are thus the key indicator of their occupation across Near and out to Remote Oceania. While pottery is one of the most researched aspects of Lapita material culture, I am focusing this discussion on the subsistence and settlement strategies that facilitated their rather rapid spread into and beyond the islands of Near Oceania.

Perhaps the most shocking and impressive thing about the Neolithic colonozation of these islands is the pace at which it happened (Sheppard (2011)).

The Lapita Package

The initial emergence of Lapita occured in environments that would have been familiar to them. Having set off from Taiwan, the proposed semi-Oceanic migratory path would have taken them through the Philippines and into the tropical environments of Near Oceania. The resource procurement strategies needed to subsist and thrive, therefore, were established prior to their colonization of these islands. Further, harkening back to the prior discussion, the islands in and around the Bismarck Arhipelago (often considered the Lapita homeland) had been at least periodically occupied for thousands of years prior to their intrusion. Evidence suggests that Lapita people interacted with indigenous populations in Near Oceania, likely learning about the new landscapes and their resources as they did so (Bedford et al. (2024); Kinaston et al. (2015); Kirch (2010)).

Subsistence

Unsurprisingly, a large contribution to the Lapita success is attributable to their maritime abilities. This, of course, includes the ability to island hop but, perhaps more important, also includes their proficiency in extracting marine resources. Fishing and shellfish foraging were paramount in Lapita subsistence. Technology that helped to facilitate this subsistence strategy includes fishhooks, pumice floats, drop line and gill nets, spears, and traps (Bedford et al. (2024)). Forageable/cultivatable crops which seem to be present in the area include Taro (Colocasia esculenta), Giant Taro (Alocasia macrorrhizos), Coconut (Cocos nucifera), Banana (Musa), Tropical Almonds (Terminalia catappa), Canarium Nuts (Canarium spp.), and Figs (Ficus spp.)(Burley et al. (2020); Horrocks et al. (2009); Kinaston et al. (2015)). Many of these resources, while known to be common to the region, are thought to have been propagated from wild species by the pre-Lapita populations of Near Oceania (evidenced from remains and artifacts associated with New Guinea). With likely input from indigenous knowledge, the early Lapita were then able to establish roots and build their distinct cultural package.

Notably, it does seem that the original wave of Austronesian speakers brought aspects of their homeland with them into Near Oceania. Domesticate animals are a wildly important resource for populations venturing into a new area. Animals such as pigs and chickens are fairly easy to transport, breed, and feed (omnivores/scavengers). They can consume many resources that are either non-digestable, not palatable, or too difficult to accumulate and turn those into preserved fat and protein. In terms of chickens, of course, these resources are also redistributed into a nice, readily consumable package (eggs). Pigs and chickens have deep roots in East and Southeast Asia, with strong evidence suggesting that they were intentionally brought to the islands as Lapita continued to expand (Stanton et al. (2026), Storey et al. (2010); Thomson et al. (2014); van Os et al. (2025)). As further proof of interaction, it is also apparent that these animals were included in some form of exchange network between the emergent Lapita and the local indigenous populations (Kirch (2019); Sheppard (2011); Thomas et al. (2020)).

While many of these resources and subsistence strategies were carried over from the initial Austronesian expansion, it was in the Bismarck Archipelago that they coalesced as part of the Lapita Package. We discuss the fairly rapid settling and colonization of Remote Oceania below. You will then come to find that a major component of this cultural spread was due to the Lapita ability to bring this package with them and essentially terraform the pristine island landscapes of Remote Oceania. Excusing the somewhat sci-fi terminology, this notion of a transported landscape is a key focus in Lapita research, explaining how they were able to venture into untouched territories with unfamiliar habitats and transform them into habitable island environments (Bedford et al. (2024); Smith (1995); Valentin et al. (2010)). The strategies employed early in the Bismarcks were ultimately enhanced and carried from island to island. While there remains some question as to whether the early Lapita of Near Oceania had fully harnessed the intensive agricultural and horticultural production of these crops, research shows that by the time they reached the islands around Tonga, they had brought with them complete, systematic cultivation strategies for crop production (Burley et al. (2020)).

Settlement

Some early insights into settlement structure could make it appear, contrary to the above, that inland resources were a negligible component of the Lapita subsistence strategy. Living areas seemed to be positioned almost exclusively along the coasts, often constructed on stilts over shallow lagoons (Burley et al. (2020); Kirch (2010)). Massive refuse mounds comprised of fishbones and harvested shellfish illustrate a clear and heavy reliance on marine resources which, along with the absence of habitation sites inland, suggested that Lapita people lived coastal beachcomber lifestyles (considered as the Strandlooper model) (Valentin et al. (2010)). To be sure, the development of stilt-house settlements in Near Oceania is a strong indicator of the emphasis placed on maritime resources but it does not imply an avoidance of inland environments. As discussed above, the tree crops that were already established across the islands were significant for daily intake and were largely carried with migrating populations to new islands. The inland environment of a tropical island can be tricky to live in, however, even if you do rely on resources from it. Certainly, space is at a premium and the clearing of land is mostly utilized for crop propagation. Further, living inland in the tropics increases one’s proximity to biting bugs and increased potential to contract disease. One of the huge benefits of building houses which are suspended above shallow coastal waters and lagoons is that it keeps them in windblown environments distinctly distant from the breeding grounds of pests (as well as limiting sanitation issues) (Burley et al. (2020)). While seemingly unrelated to our discussion of the Lapita transported landscape, the thoughtful construction of communities in sanitary parts of these islands is a major contributing factor to their rapid advance through Oceania.
